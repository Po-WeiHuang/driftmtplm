\section{Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

This research investigates latent representation alignment in
policy distillation, aiming to improve multi-token generation
beyond supervision based solely on token-level predictions.
While conventional knowledge distillation primarily constrains
output distributions, the geometry of intermediate latent
representations remains underdetermined.


Our goal is to establish how latent geometric alignment
can improve the accuracy--efficiency trade-off in
multi-token prediction. To this end, we develop a
geometry-aware drifting regularizer that aligns student
and teacher latent-state distributions, and compare it
with conventional token-level supervision and paired
feature regression. We will like to demonstrate that population-level drifting offers advantages over pointwise regression and token-level alignment in terms of better thoughput and latency while maintaining or improving reasoning accuracy. 




\section{Novelty}
\label{sec:novelty}

Our contributions are twofold: introducing drifting
regularization~\citep{deng2026drifting} as a latent-space
supervision objective for multi-token prediction (MTP)
self-distillation, and systematically investigating how
its attraction--repulsion components, sampling strategies,
and hyperparameters influence representation alignment
and generation performance.

First, we adapt drifting to language-model hidden
representations, where teacher states serve as positive
samples and other student states form the negative
population. The resulting attraction--repulsion field
encourages population-level alignment without enforcing
exact pointwise correspondence, providing an alternative
to conventional paired feature regression
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

Second, we decompose the drifting formulation into three
components to examine their respective contributions
and identify suitable sampling strategies
(Section~\ref{sec:self-negative}). We further investigate
how these design choices affect the alignment of
teacher and student feature distributions and their
relationship to multi-token rollout performance.

\section{Impact}
\label{sec:impact}

We hypothesize that improved latent representation alignment
can enhance multi-token prediction beyond the gains achievable
through token-level self-distillation alone. In particular,
preserving the teacher's representation geometry may help
the student maintain coherent and distinguishable predictive
states across longer prediction horizons, potentially improving
generation quality without sacrificing parallelism.

The proposed approach could enable the model to produce
more reliable tokens per forward pass, thereby reducing
generation latency and increasing token throughput under
standalone MTP inference. Since drifting regularization is
applied only during training, it introduces no additional
inference module or verification stage.

More broadly, this study aims to clarify the relationship
between representation geometry, token-level prediction
quality, and the accuracy--acceleration trade-off in
self-distilled language models. Such findings could inform
the design of representation-level supervision for other
parallel generation and model distillation frameworks.

\subsection{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:mtp}

Multi-token prediction (MTP) via self-distillation by 
\citet{kirchenbauer2026mtp} converts a pretrained
autoregressive language model into a standalone multi-token
predictor through on-policy self-distillation. The pretrained
model serves as a frozen teacher, while a student initialized
from the same checkpoint learns to predict multiple future
tokens simultaneously. The teacher evaluates the student's
proposed continuation autoregressively, providing token-level
supervision without requiring an additional verification model
during inference.

During the training, for each region $r$, a prefix $c_r$ is followed by $k_r-1$ trainable \texttt{<MTP>} mask tokens, allowing the student to propose $k_r$ future tokens in one forward pass. Repeated position IDs and blocked causal attention preserve the original context computation while allowing each prediction state to attend to the prefix and preceding masks. At horizon $j$, the student produces latent state $u^S_{r,j}$ and distribution $p^S_{r,j}$, yielding a greedy proposal $\hat y_{r,j}=\arg\max_v p^S_{r,j}(v)$.

The teacher then supplies targets on the continuation actually proposed by the student, rather than on a fixed ground-truth continuation. With a frozen autoregressive teacher,
\begin{equation}
 u^T_{r,j}=H_{\theta_T}(c_r,\hat y_{r,<j}),\qquad
 \bar y_{r,j}=\arg\max_v p_{\theta_T}(v\mid c_r,\hat y_{r,<j}).
\end{equation}
The hard-teacher token objective is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{MTP}}=-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{act}}}\sum_{r,j}
 \log p^S_{r,j}(\bar y_{r,j}),\qquad N_{\mathrm{act}}=\sum_r k_r.
\end{equation}
This common token objective is retained in every experimental family so that changes in rollout behaviour can be attributed more clearly to the added representation losses.

\paragraph{Paired self-distillation and self-negative correction}
\label{sec:self-negative}

The construction of the positive and negative pools
$\mathcal J_i^+$ and $\mathcal J_i^-$ requires careful
consideration of sample selection. In particular, including
the student anchor itself in the negative pool or retaining
its paired teacher representation in the positive pool
can introduce undesirable weighting effects. We therefore
introduce a correction to mitigate these issues which will be introduced at the end of this section.


Directly including the student anchor $x_i$ in the
negative population can cause its self-affinity
$\kappa_h(x_i,x_i)=1$ to dominate the normalized negative
weights, despite producing zero displacement.
Conversely, including the paired teacher $y_i^+$ in
the positive population while excluding $x_i$ from
the negative population introduces an asymmetric
weighting of paired and unpaired interactions.

To characterize this asymmetry, we define the normalized
paired-teacher affinity $a_i$, the weighted mean $P_i$
of other teacher features, and the weighted mean $N_i$
of other student features:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a_i &= a_{ii}^{+},\\
P_i &= \frac{1}{1-a_i}
       \sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{+}y_j^{+},\\
N_i &= \sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{-}x_j.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:population_means}
\end{equation}
Here, $a_{ij}^{+}$ and $a_{ij}^{-}$ are the
row-normalized teacher and student affinities,
respectively, with the student diagonal excluded
from $a_{ij}^{-}$. The definition of $P_i$ applies
when $a_i<1$; otherwise its weighted contribution
vanishes.

The resulting drifting field admits a three-term
decomposition:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat V_i^{\mathrm{masked}}
={}&
\underbrace{a_i(y_i^+-x_i)}_{\text{paired attraction}}\\
&+
\underbrace{(1-a_i)(P_i-N_i)}_{\text{population correction}}\\
&+
\underbrace{a_i(x_i-N_i)}_{\text{residual repulsion}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:masked_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term attracts the student towards its
paired teacher, while the second captures the
difference between the remaining teacher and
student populations. The third term is an additional
repulsive contribution caused by the mismatch in
normalization: unpaired teacher features carry
total weight $1-a_i$, whereas other student features
retain unit total weight.

To eliminate this residual term, we introduce a
\emph{tied self-negative} construction that assigns
the student anchor the same negative weight as its

\begin{equation}
\widetilde a_{ij}^{-}=
\begin{cases}
a_i, & j=i,\\
(1-a_i)a_{ij}^{-}, & j\neq i.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tied_negative}
\end{equation}
This preserves the normalization
$\sum_j\widetilde a_{ij}^{-}=1$
while maintaining the relative affinities among
other student features. The resulting field becomes
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\widehat V_i^{\mathrm{tied}}
=
a_i(y_i^+-x_i)
+
(1-a_i)(P_i-N_i).
}
\label{eq:tied_drift}
\end{equation}
Although the self-negative contributes no direct
displacement, its assigned weight reduces the
repulsive contribution from other student states.
Under exact pointwise teacher--student matching,
the tied field vanishes, unlike the diagonal-masked
formulation.

\subsection{Evaluation: from feature geometry to rollout performance}
\label{sec:evaluation}
A geometric improvement matters for this proposal only if its relationship to generation can be measured. We therefore separate latent diagnostics from end-to-end rollout outcomes. The current pilot uses MetaMathQA for training and GSM8K for free-rollout evaluation; broader benchmarks will be chosen for larger checkpoints. The frozen autoregressive teacher under sequential decoding ($k=1$) is the capability-retention reference.

Feature spread, maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)~\citep{gretton2012kernel}, and Sinkhorn transport cost~\citep{cuturi2013sinkhorn} assess different aspects of teacher--student geometry.We also evaluate token-level expected calibration error
(ECE)~\citep{joshi2025calibration} to assess how closely
the model's predicted token confidence reflects its
empirical prediction accuracy. All metrics are reported separately for each prediction
horizon to isolate horizon-dependent effects and avoid
confounding trends arising from aggregating predictions
across different horizons.
Free-rollout accuracy is compared against the effective number of emitted tokens per decoding pass,
\begin{equation}
 k_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\text{emitted non-padding tokens}}
 {\text{decoding forward passes}}.
\end{equation}
Both fixed-$k$ and confidence-adaptive decoding will be assessed. Measured tokens per second and end-to-end latency will be reported separately, because $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is a proxy for acceleration rather than a wall-clock speedup.

\section{Risks}
\label{sec:risks}
\paragraph{The wrong neighbours can give the wrong drift.}
A teacher feature may be close in Euclidean distance without representing a comparable prediction context. Small or heterogeneous pools could therefore push the student in unhelpful directions, especially at longer horizons. We will inspect pool composition and kernel weights by horizon, vary bandwidth and pool size, and omit updates when too few usable neighbours are available.

\paragraph{A better-looking feature space may not generate better answers.}
The pilot already shows why feature distances cannot be the sole success criterion: SmoothL1 and drifting change spread and transport cost differently. Strong regularization may also damage the token objective. We will compare methods at similar auxiliary gradient strengths and judge improvements primarily by free-rollout accuracy at matched effective $k$, followed by measured throughput and latency.

\paragraph{Training overhead versus inference gains.}
Drifting introduces additional computational overhead
during training through pairwise feature interactions,
which may limit its practicality at larger model scales.
We will measure training wall-clock time to quantify
this additional cost and compare inference latency
and token throughput across different training objectives.
These measurements will establish whether improvements
in generation quality and inference efficiency justify
the additional training cost.ference gain.

\paragraph{Results on one maths benchmark may not transfer.}
The current evidence comes from MetaMathQA-trained models evaluated on GSM8K. It does not establish benefits for other reasoning tasks or open-ended generation. Larger-model evaluation will therefore include held-out tasks beyond GSM8K, with benchmark overlap checked and model-selection data kept separate from final tests.

\section{Timeline \& Resource Requirements}
\label{sec:timeline-resources}
The baseline reproduction is complete, and the current work is moving from 1B pilot diagnostics to controlled hyperparameter selection and larger-model validation. The remaining phases depend on compute availability.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Completed:} reproduce on-policy MTP, establish the frozen-teacher reference, and validate free-rollout evaluation.
 \item \textbf{Ongoing:} compare drifting, SmoothL1, and combined losses at 1B; examine feature geometry and the effects of kernel bandwidth and training horizon.
 \item \textbf{Next:} use an 8B model to select hyperparameters under matched training budgets, with repeated evaluations and additional reasoning benchmarks.
 \item \textbf{Final study:} transfer the selected settings to a 32B mixture-of-experts (MoE) model and measure accuracy, effective $k$, throughput, and latency.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Computing resource request.}
For 2 billion training tokens and a frozen teacher, we estimate approximately 10 hours of idealized compute for an 8B dense model using four NVIDIA B200 GPUs at 40\% model FLOPs utilization. We request access to four B200 GPUs for approximately two days per 8B configuration to allow for MTP-specific overhead, evaluation, and profiling. Multiple configurations and seeds would require additional allocations. The final 32B MoE run will be budgeted after profiling its active-parameter compute and full-model memory requirements. Access to GPUs, storage, and large-model training support from the University of Oxford Department of Computer Science and the MediaTek Computing Research Centre would help complete the proposed comparisons.
